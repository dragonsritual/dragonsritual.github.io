Building a World That Remembers

The first real question is not how large the world should be. It is whether the places inside it can remember what happened there.

I have been thinking about what makes an old fantasy world feel believable. It is rarely the number of quests, enemies, or map tiles. It is the sense that the world existed before the player arrived and continues to carry the marks left behind.

A tree should not simply disappear when it is cut. A stump remains. The logs fall in the direction of the strike. A clearing slowly becomes a camp, then a shelter, then perhaps a settlement with its own history.

The goal is not merely to build a large map. The goal is to create places that begin to belong to the player.

That principle now guides the work: gathering, construction, followers, weather, creatures, roads, abandoned buildings, and every system that will eventually touch the wilderness.

The Shape of the Wilderness

Trees, stones, water, and grass are only the beginning. The wilderness needs recognizable places and useful memories.

The earliest version of the world contains clustered trees, stone deposits, ponds, workers, and simple gathering. The next stage is to make those systems feel connected rather than placed beside one another.

19 JULY 2026 · WORKSHOP LOG · ENTRY 003

Separating Tools from Weapons

A woodcutting axe and a battle axe may share a silhouette, but they should not share the same purpose, rhythm, or animation.

Working animations belong to professions and resources. Combat animations belong to weapons and danger. Keeping those systems separate now will make both of them stronger later.
